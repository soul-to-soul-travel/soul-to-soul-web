> Working document · August 2026

Lineamientos de qué debe comunicar cada página de viaje (journey page) de RAÍZ, cómo debe comunicarlo, y la jerarquía de contenido recomendada. Esta es la base sobre la que se construye el contenido de las páginas de viaje del sitio.

---

## 1. Core principle

A RAÍZ journey page should sell the desire to experience a territory, not simply explain an itinerary. The page needs to work on two levels at the same time: **emotional desire** and **practical clarity**.

- **Visual first:** photography should help the traveler imagine what it feels like to be there, not only what the destination looks like.
- **Experience before logistics:** communicate culture, nature, food, craft, human connection, pace and atmosphere before reducing the journey to a schedule.
- **Clarity early:** dates, duration, price and the essential trip facts should be easy to understand without extensive scrolling.
- **Premium without luxury clichés:** communicate care, access, curation, intimacy and quality rather than status language.
- **Human and territorial:** the journey should feel created by people who know and care about the place.

## 2. Recommended page hierarchy

The exact visual layout is still a design decision, but the content should broadly follow this sequence.

### 01 — Hero / First impression

- Lead with one strong image or a small visual composition that immediately establishes the territory and the emotional tone of the journey.
- Use a short journey title and a concise line that captures its essence. The title can reference the territory and/or the character of the experience.
- Avoid generic destination photography and overly busy text overlays. Photography should have room to speak.

### 02 — Essential trip information

- This information should appear very high on the page and be immediately scannable.
- **Dates:** show the fixed departure dates when applicable. For flexible journeys, use language aligned with RAÍZ such as "Open dates" or the final custom-made terminology once defined.
- **Duration:** nights and/or days.
- **Price:** show the starting price and clearly state the pricing basis, for example "based on two people sharing".
- **Primary CTA:** a warm, low-friction invitation to continue the conversation rather than a transactional "Buy Now".

### 03 — Journey introduction

- A short editorial introduction explaining what this journey is, what territory it reveals, and why RAÍZ believes it is worth experiencing.
- This should not become a long introduction to Colombia or a destination encyclopedia. If broader "Why Colombia" content is needed, it can live elsewhere and be linked from the journey page.
- Allow the traveler to recognize whether the journey is for them: for example, a love of mountains, coffee culture, living traditions, nature, calm, food or craft. This should feel integrated into the narrative rather than like a generic checklist.

### 04 — Experience Highlights / Key Experiences

- Place this **before** the detailed itinerary.
- Use a concise set of memorable experiences or defining moments to give the traveler an immediate sense of what makes this journey distinctive.
- These are not day-by-day activities. They are the experiences a traveler is likely to remember and talk about afterwards.

### 05 — Journey value propositions

- Consider **3 concise pillars** that summarize the character of the journey — inspired by benchmark examples such as "Rich History & Culture / Local Connections / Authentic Flavors".
- For RAÍZ these pillars should be specific to each journey: craftsmanship, biodiversity, coffee culture, mountain life, food, wellbeing, local connection, etc.
- Do not use the same three pillars mechanically across every journey.

### 06 — What to Expect

- This is a key RAÍZ section and should go beyond what the traveler will *see*.
- Communicate what the journey may *feel* like: pace, atmosphere, encounters, sensory moments, conversations and opportunities for reflection.
- Include the deeper dimension of the experience when it is authentic to the journey — for example, an evening fireside conversation, time for reflection, or a meaningful closing moment.
- The language should remain grounded and specific. Avoid spirituality or transformation claims that feel forced.

### 07 — Itinerary

- Present the itinerary with enough detail for the traveler to imagine each day, but without turning it into an operational schedule.
- Prefer short narrative descriptions over long activity lists.
- Include details that add meaning: a family-run restaurant, a craft tradition, the character of a landscape, the reason a meal or encounter matters.
- Do not disclose unnecessary operational detail or exact hotel names by default. Accommodation can be described by character, e.g. "boutique countryside hotel".
- Use photography throughout the itinerary so the page continues to feel visual and editorial. A small illustrated map can help orient the traveler without dominating the page.

### 08 — What's Included / Additional Expenses

- Make inclusions easy to understand. The final visual format is still to be resolved: grouped categories versus a clean list.
- Keep the emphasis on what **is** included. "Not included" can be short and visually secondary when the exclusions are straightforward.
- Possible categories include accommodation & dining, guided experiences, transportation/logistics and other journey-specific inclusions.
- Consider "Additional expenses" as a softer alternative to a large "What's not included" block when appropriate.

### 09 — Important Information

- Keep practical information concise and relevant to the specific journey.
- Examples: arrival/departure point, altitude, meaningful physical requirements, accessibility considerations, weather or permits when genuinely relevant.
- Do not create a large "Good to know" section simply because competitors have one. Global questions belong in the FAQ.

### 10 — Trip Impact

- Explore a concise section explaining where the traveler's spend creates positive local value.
- This can include local guides, artisans, independent producers, community initiatives or cultural practices supported through the journey.
- Avoid broad sustainability claims. The communication should be specific and evidence-based.

### 11 — Journey Creator / Travel Designer

- This should appear toward the lower part of every journey page and is a content decision, not merely a design device.
- Put a face and a person behind the journey. Include a portrait, name, role and a short description.
- Most importantly, explain how that person is connected to the territory: where the relationship comes from, what they know or love about the place, and why they were the right person to create this particular journey.
- The copy should make authorship meaningful rather than presenting a generic staff bio. The final label — "Journey Creator", "Travel Designer", "Journey Author" or another term — remains to be defined.

### 12 — Social proof, FAQ and next step

- Use relevant testimonials where available, preferably ones that speak to the quality of the experience rather than generic praise.
- Include only journey-specific FAQs on the journey page; broader booking, safety, payment and travel questions can link to the global FAQ.
- End with a clear CTA to check dates, enquire or start planning. CTAs should also appear naturally at key moments earlier in the page rather than only at the bottom.

## 3. How RAÍZ should communicate

- Write with an **editorial, warm and confident voice**. The language should feel considered but never overwritten.
- Describe lived experience rather than using tourism verbs repeatedly. Avoid formulaic language such as "pay a visit to…".
- Use sensory and human detail when it adds meaning: hands at work, food being prepared, a conversation, the sound or pace of a place.
- Describe Colombia with nuance and confidence. Avoid repeatedly "demystifying" the country inside every itinerary; broader destination positioning can live in a dedicated Why Colombia context.
- Keep paragraphs short and scannable. The page should have rhythm: image → practical information → experience → image.
- Avoid excessive use of "luxury". Premium quality should be felt through the curation, service, access, accommodation, photography and writing.
- Avoid transactional language such as "Shop Trips". The page should invite exploration and conversation.
- No generic emojis or visual devices that weaken the editorial character of the brand.

## 4. Photography principles

- The journey is sold visually. Photography is not decoration; it is part of the narrative.
- Use more than landscapes. Include people, hands, artisans, food, materials, interiors, rituals, movement, quiet moments and everyday life.
- Choose images that communicate what it feels like to be in the territory, even when they are less "perfect" than conventional tourism photography.
- Favor an atmospheric, editorial, calm visual language with coherent treatment across the page.
- Avoid low-resolution images, inconsistent filters, overly saturated travel imagery and image crops that feel accidental.
- Use enough photography throughout long pages so the experience never becomes a wall of text.

## 5. What to avoid

- Long destination essays before the traveler understands the journey.
- Rigid day-by-day schedules full of operational detail.
- Hotel disclosure when it is not strategically necessary.
- Repetition of dates, price and practical information in multiple sections without purpose.
- Activity level as a prominent summary metric unless the journey genuinely requires it; physical demands can be explained where relevant.
- Too much text, oversized typography that consumes the screen, or large empty sections that interrupt reading rhythm.
- Too few CTAs or CTAs that appear only at the very end.
- Generic luxury vocabulary, generic sustainability claims and generic destination copy.
- Photography that is technically attractive but emotionally disconnected from the experience.

## 6. Decisions already established vs. still to resolve

### Established

- Essential information (dates, duration and price) appears near the top.
- Experience Highlights / Key Experiences appear before the itinerary.
- A dedicated What to Expect section is part of the journey page.
- The itinerary is narrative and concise rather than operational.
- The page should be highly visual and photography should communicate feeling as well as place.
- A Journey Creator / Travel Designer section appears toward the bottom and explains the creator's personal relationship with the territory.
- Calls to action appear at multiple natural points.
- Practical information and FAQs remain concise and relevant.

### Still to resolve

- Final terminology for flexible dates ("Open dates", "Custom-made", etc.).
- Final CTA wording.
- Final label for Journey Creator / Travel Designer / Journey Author.
- Exact visual format for What's Included and how prominently to show exclusions.
- Whether "Trip Impact" becomes a standard section or appears only when there is a meaningful story to tell.
- Exact number and naming of journey value propositions.
- How much itinerary detail is ideal before inquiry.
- Final treatment of accommodation information and whether any hotel names are ever revealed pre-inquiry.

## 7. Working content flow

Hero / visual statement → Essential information + CTA → Journey introduction → Experience Highlights → Journey value propositions → What to Expect → Itinerary + photography/map → What's Included → Important Information → Trip Impact (when relevant) → Journey Creator / Travel Designer → Testimonials / FAQ → Final CTA.

The order can be adjusted during design, but the principle should remain: **inspire first, clarify quickly, deepen the experience, then make the next step easy.**
